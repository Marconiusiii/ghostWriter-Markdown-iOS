# Chapter one: The unopened parcel

Mara found the parcel beside the kitchen door when she returned from the station. It was wrapped in brown paper and tied with cotton string, with her name written across the front in her brother's careful handwriting. He had been away for nearly a year. Until that morning, every message from him had arrived on a postcard with barely enough room for a greeting.

She put the kettle on before opening it. Inside was a small notebook, a folded street map, and a key attached to a wooden label. The notebook smelled faintly of the cedar drawer where their father had kept his papers. Mara recognized it immediately, although she could not remember ever seeing anything written inside.

## The letter

A letter lay between the notebook's first two pages. Her brother had found a tenant for their father's old workshop, but he wanted Mara to visit before the agreement was signed. There were things in the building that belonged to both of them, and things that neither had thought about for years.

He had enclosed a list of practical questions: whether to keep the workbench, what to do with the shelves, and who might want the collection of hand tools. At the bottom he had added one request that did not fit with the others. Would she look in the blue cupboard before deciding anything? Mara read that sentence twice. Their father had owned many cupboards, but she remembered only one painted blue, a narrow cabinet beside the window where he stored unfinished projects. As children they had been allowed to open every drawer in the workshop except its bottom drawer. They had invented explanations for the restriction until the explanations became more interesting than whatever might actually be inside. Now she had the key, the cupboard still existed, and there was no one left to ask whether the rule had ever mattered. She folded the letter along its original creases and put it in her coat pocket. The questions about shelves and tools could wait until she had seen the room again.

### A decision before breakfast

The next train left in forty minutes. Mara filled a flask, found a bag large enough for the notebook, and wrote a short reply to her brother. She would visit the workshop that afternoon. She made no promises about the cupboard.

By the time she reached the station, the rain had stopped. A porter was sweeping leaves away from the ticket machine. Everything about the journey seemed ordinary, which made the key in her pocket feel stranger than it had at home.

# Chapter two: The workshop

The street had changed less than Mara expected. The bakery was now a bicycle shop, and the pavement outside the school had been widened, but the workshop still stood behind the same low wall. She unlocked the side gate and followed the path to the door.

Inside, the room was cool and quiet. Someone had covered the workbench with a clean sheet. The shelves were labeled in her brother's handwriting, and three empty boxes waited beneath them. He had clearly done the easy sorting and left the difficult decisions for another day.

## The blue cupboard

The cupboard door opened without resistance. On the top shelf were unfinished wooden toys, each wrapped in a piece of cloth. Beneath them was a stack of envelopes. The bottom drawer contained no secret machinery, no valuable collection, and none of the treasures Mara had imagined as a child.

Instead, she found a row of notebooks like the one her brother had sent. Each covered a year of their father's work. Some pages recorded measurements or the cost of timber; others described conversations with customers, repairs that had gone wrong, or improvements he wanted to try. Mara sat on a stool and began with the oldest volume. A note about replacing a chair leg led into an account of the woman who had brought it in. A sketch of a window frame shared a page with the route of a walk along the river. Near the middle of the book, she found a description of a wooden boat he had made for her seventh birthday. He had written about the trouble of balancing its sail and the afternoon they tested it in a basin behind the house. She remembered the boat capsizing. He remembered her insisting that it needed a passenger. Neither version was the whole story, but together they brought back an afternoon she had not thought about in decades.

### What to keep

Mara placed the notebooks in her bag. The workbench could stay with the new tenant, and the tools could go to someone who would use them. She would ask her brother about the toys before taking any home.

She left the blue cupboard where it was. It belonged to the room, with its uneven floor and its window overlooking the wall. What she wanted to keep fitted into the bag at her feet.

# Chapter three: The reply

On the journey home, Mara wrote a longer letter. She answered the questions about the shelves and workbench first. Then she described the notebooks, including the story of the boat, and suggested that they read the rest together.

Her brother called before she reached her stop. He had been waiting to hear whether she had opened the drawer. For a while they talked about the workshop as if they were standing in it, moving from one remembered object to another.

## A place for the notebooks

At home, Mara cleared a shelf beside her desk. She arranged the volumes by year and left the oldest open at the page about the boat. There would be time to read them slowly, without turning each discovery into another task to finish.

The key went into an envelope for the new tenant. Before sealing it, she removed the wooden label and set it beside the books. Her father's handwriting had faded, but the single word on it was still clear: workshop.
